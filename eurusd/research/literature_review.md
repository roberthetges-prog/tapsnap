# EUR/USD Predictability: Literature Review

Compiled 2026-11-10. Scope: peer-reviewed and central-bank research on predictable behaviour in EUR/USD and major FX, with testable definitions and reported effect sizes.

**Source caveat.** In this session no full-text host (bis.org, nber.org, ssrn.com, arxiv.org, ecb.europa.eu, federalreserve.gov) resolved, so every figure below comes from abstracts, working-paper excerpts and citing papers surfaced by web search (~58 distinct queries). Where a paper's tables were not reachable the entry says **not reported**. Nothing is invented. Verify magnitudes against the full text before hard-coding them.

Legend for each bullet: authors (year), title, venue | sample | rule/definition | effect size | significance | transaction costs | **Implication**.

## 1. Technical trading rules in FX

- **Levich & Thomas (1993)**, "The significance of technical trading-rule profits in the foreign exchange market: a bootstrap approach", *J. International Money & Finance* (NBER WP 3818). | IMM currency futures, 1976–1990, five currencies incl. DM. | x% filter: buy when price rises x% above most recent trough, short when it falls x% below most recent peak; dual moving-average: buy when short MA crosses long MA from below. Exact x and MA lengths: not reported in sources reached. | Profits "highly unusual" vs 10,000 bootstrap shuffles; MA rules more profitable than filters. Annual % per rule: not reported. | Bootstrap p-values significant for most rules in full sample; declined but remained positive/significant in many cases in the 1986–90 sub-period. | Secondary source: annual costs ~1.6–2.6% eliminated profitability of some rules. | **Implication**: the canonical 1970s–80s trend-rule result; treat as historical baseline, not live edge.
- **Neely, Weller & Dittmar (1997)**, "Is Technical Analysis in the Foreign Exchange Market Profitable? A Genetic Programming Approach", *JFQA* 32(4):405–426. | Six dollar rates, out-of-sample 1981–1995. | Rules evolved by genetic programming on daily data; in-sample 1975–80, selection 1981–?, OOS thereafter. | "Economically significant" OOS excess returns for each of six rates; magnitudes not reported in sources reached. | Bootstrap on $/DM rejects standard null models. | Costs included (one-way cost per trade, value not reported). | **Implication**: evolved rules beat canned rules historically; the same authors later (2009) show the effect decayed.
- **LeBaron (1999)**, "Technical trading rule profitability and foreign exchange intervention", *J. International Economics* 49(1):125–143. | $/DM and $/yen, daily/weekly, 1979–1992 (per WP). | 150-day MA rule (daily; 30-week weekly): long when price > MA, else short. | Removing weeks of Fed intervention "dramatically reduces" predictability; Sharpe not reported. | Significant only when intervention periods included. | Not reported. | **Implication**: MA-rule profits were an intervention artefact; central banks no longer intervene in EUR/USD, so expect nothing.
- **Okunev & White (2003)**, "Do Momentum-Based Strategies Still Work in Foreign Currency Markets?", *JFQA* 38(2):425–447. | 8 currencies, 1980–2000. | Rank currencies by MA-crossover signal (short MA minus long MA), long top / short bottom; insensitive to MA lengths and base currency. | Significantly positive excess returns, persisting through the 1990s; magnitude not reported in sources reached. | Significant. | Not reported. | **Implication**: cross-sectional MA ranking (needs a basket) outlived single-pair MA rules.
- **Qi & Wu (2006)**, "Technical Trading-Rule Profitability, Data Snooping, and Reality Check: Evidence from the Foreign Exchange Market", *JMCB* 38(8):2135–2158. | 2,127 rules, seven dollar rates, 1973–1998 (per citing papers). | Filter, MA, support/resistance, channel breakouts, oscillators; White's Reality Check. | Full sample survives Reality Check; "profitability becomes much weaker in the more recent period". | Full-sample RC p-values significant; second half much weaker. | Yes (break-even costs discussed; values not reported). | **Implication**: survives data-snooping correction only in the pre-1990s half.
- **Neely, Weller & Ulrich (2009)**, "The Adaptive Markets Hypothesis: Evidence from the Foreign Exchange Market", *JFQA* 44(2):467–488. | Rules from earlier published studies, re-tested OOS to ~2005. | Filter rules, MA rules, channel breakouts, genetically-programmed rules, Markov rules. | 1970s–80s excess returns were genuine (not data mining); **filter and MA profits vanished by the early 1990s**; less-studied rules declined but "probably not completely disappeared". | OOS t-stats for filter/MA insignificant after ~1990. | Yes. | **Implication**: simple MA/filter on EUR/USD is dead; only novel/less-studied rules may retain small edges.
- **Hsu, Taylor & Wang (2016)**, "Technical trading: Is it still beating the foreign exchange market?", *J. International Economics* 102:188–208 (SSRN 2765673). | Daily, 45 years, 30 developed + emerging currencies vs USD. | 21,195 rules (filter, MA, support-resistance, channel breakout, oscillator); stepwise SPA test for data snooping; OOS cross-validation. | Excerpts: one selected rule set averages 4.7% p.a. excess return, Sharpe 0.852; another reports OOS Sharpe 1.81, mean 7.3% p.a. — the currency group for each figure is not identified in the excerpts. Abstract: excess profitability for both developed and emerging currencies, attributed to "temporarily not-fully-rational behaviour and market immaturity". | Stepwise-SPA significant. | Break-even costs reported (values not reached). | **Implication**: the one modern paper claiming continued profitability; emerging markets drive much of it. Treat developed-market (EUR/USD) results as weak until replicated.
- Later/contrary: an NYU working paper (undated) on technical rules in currency markets reports declining profitability; HSBC (Euromoney) optimised FX momentum Sharpe ~0.35, simple MA variant ~−0.05 post-crisis (sell-side, not peer-reviewed).

## 2. Momentum and reversal

- **Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling & Schrimpf (2012)**, "Currency momentum strategies", *JFE* 106(3):660–684 (BIS WP 366). | 48 currencies vs USD, 1976–2010, monthly. | Cross-sectional: sort on past f-month excess return (f = 1,3,6,9,12), hold h months; long top / short bottom (6 portfolios). | Winner-minus-loser spread "up to 10% p.a." (1-month formation / 1-month holding strongest); momentum concentrated in high-cost, high-vol, high-country-risk currencies. | Highly significant gross. | Bid-ask adjustment "lowers profitability significantly" but costs "cannot completely account" for it; net figures not reported in sources reached. | **Implication**: a basket effect; for a single major pair the exploitable part after spreads is small and unquantified.
- **Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (2012)**, "Time series momentum", *JFE* 104(2):228–250. | 58 futures/forwards incl. 12 FX pairs (9 currencies), 1985–2009. | Sign of past 12-month excess return, hold 1 month, position scaled to 40% ex-ante annualised vol. | Positive for every one of 58 contracts; FX-only Sharpe not reported in sources reached; diversified portfolio Sharpe ~1.3 (Quantpedia summary). | Significant pooled t-stats for lookbacks 1–12 months. | Discussed; FX costs low. | **Implication**: 12-month TSMOM is the most replicated trend result; EUR/USD alone will have a much lower Sharpe than the basket.
- **Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen (AQR, 2014/2017)**, "A Century of Evidence on Trend-Following Investing". | 67 markets incl. currencies, 1880–2013 (2017 update to 2016). | Equal-weight 1-, 3-, 12-month TSMOM. | Full-sample net-of-fee Sharpe 0.77 for the combined portfolio; currency-only not reported. | Not a formal test. | Net of 2/20 fees and costs. | **Implication**: trend is robust across a century but the per-asset Sharpe is low; EUR/USD stand-alone expectation ~0.2–0.4 (inference, not reported).
- **Burnside, Eichenbaum & Rebelo (2011)**, "Carry Trade and Momentum in Currency Markets", *Annual Review of Financial Economics* (NBER WP 16942). | 20 currencies, 1976–2010. | Momentum: long currencies with positive prior-month excess return, short negative; carry: long high-rate, short low-rate. | Momentum 4.4% p.a., sd 7.3%, Sharpe 0.60; carry 4.5–4.6% p.a., sd 5.1–5.2%, Sharpe 0.86–0.89; 50/50 blend Sharpe 0.98; US equities Sharpe ~0.41 same period. | Significant. | Bid-ask included. | **Implication**: 1-month momentum and carry are roughly uncorrelated; combine.
- **Dao (2016)**, "A calendar effect: Weekend overreaction (and subsequent reversal) in spot FX rates", *J. Multinational Financial Management* 37–38:158–167. | 16 pairs (7 major, 9 EM), daily, 2002-01-01 to 2014-05-31, Bloomberg. | Large gap between Friday close and Monday open → reversal over multiple horizons in the following week. | 15 of 16 pairs show significant reversal; strategy returns not reported in sources reached. | Significant. | Not reported. | **Implication**: fade large Monday gaps on EUR/USD; verify post-2014.
- **Filippou, Taylor & Wang (2023)**, "Media Sentiment and Currency Reversals", *JFQA*. | 48 rates, 1.2m news articles. | Buy low-media-sentiment / sell high-sentiment currencies; sentiment factor negatively predicts next-month returns. | Significant; magnitude not reported. | Not reported. | **Implication**: short-term reversal in FX exists but is sentiment-conditioned, not a plain 1-week price reversal; plain short-term reversal papers for FX were not found.
- Post-GFC: a 2025 *International Review of Financial Analysis* paper ("Conditional currency momentum portfolios") reports currency momentum "has not generated positive returns after the global financial crisis"; BER note momentum made money *during* 2008. Evidence on decay is mixed.

## 3. Carry, UIP and interest differentials

- **Fama (1984)** forward-premium regression: Δs(t+1) = α + β(f−s) + ε; β < 1, often negative for advanced economies (the puzzle). **Bussière, Chinn, Ferrara & Heipertz (2018/2022)**, "The New Fama Puzzle" (NBER WP 24342): for eight dollar rates the negative β "does not survive into the period during and in the decade after the financial crisis" — β becomes large and **positive** (UIP-consistent) post-2008; survey expectations explain the shift. **Goldberg et al. (2023)** find β "more often insignificant or positive than negative" with structural instability. | **Implication**: carry-sign on EUR/USD (long the higher-yielder) is not a reliable edge post-2008; sign may even have flipped.
- **Lustig, Roussanov & Verdelhan (2014)**, "Countercyclical currency risk premia", *JFE* 111(3):527–553. | Developed-currency basket vs USD, post-1983 monthly (exact dates not reported). | "Dollar carry": position in the whole foreign basket vs USD with sign given by the average forward discount (AFD): long foreign/short USD when foreign rates exceed US rates, reversed otherwise. | Sharpe > 0.50 (higher than HML carry and US equities over same sample); AFD plus US industrial production forecast up to 25% (published; 30% in a revision) of dollar-return variation at 12 months. | Significant. | Not reported. | **Implication**: a slow (monthly–annual) dollar-direction signal usable for EUR/USD sizing.
- **Burnside et al. carry** (above): Sharpe 0.86. **CXO summary**: carry performance "poor for both developed and emerging markets since the financial crisis".
- **Ang & Chen (2010)**, "Yield Curve Predictors of Foreign Exchange Returns" (Columbia WP). | G10 vs USD, monthly. | Changes in short rates and term spreads (10y−3m) predict excess FX returns beyond carry, up to 12 months. | Portfolios on these signals have low skew and low correlation with carry; magnitudes not reported. | Significant. | Not reported. | **Implication**: rate *changes* and curve slope, not levels, carry forward information.
- **Chen & Tsang (2013)**, "What Does the Yield Curve Tell Us About Exchange Rate Predictability?", *REStat*. | Nelson-Siegel level/slope/curvature differentials predict FX changes and excess returns 1–24 months ahead. | Magnitude not reported. | **Implication**: as above.
- **2-year differential (practitioner evidence only)**: SocGen (Juckes, 2017): 10-year R² of EUR/USD on US–DE 2y spread > 0.7, "close to zero since start of 2015, sign reversed"; BBH (2017): 60-day correlation of changes 0.56; BNP (2024): high correlation again; one model explains ~5% of daily EUR/USD changes with the US 2y as the largest term. | **Implication**: a regime-dependent contemporaneous driver; weak as a daily *predictor*.

## 4. Intraday seasonality and fixes

- **Ranaldo (2009)**, "Segmentation and time-of-day patterns in foreign exchange markets", *J. Banking & Finance* 33(12):2199–2206 (SNB WP 2007-03). | Several rates, many years (not reported exactly), hourly. | Home currency depreciates during home working hours and appreciates during the counterpart's working hours; domestic agents are net buyers of foreign currency in their own hours. | "Statistically and economically highly significant"; overrides calendar effects; magnitudes not reported. | Not reported. | **Implication**: EUR weak in European hours, strong in US hours.
- **Breedon & Ranaldo (2013)**, "Intraday Patterns in FX Returns and Order Flow", *JMCB* 45(5):953–965. | Hourly spot, 6 major pairs, Jan 1997–May 2007; plus single-dealer order flow. | Currency depreciates during its **local** trading hours (no significant pattern during foreign hours, unlike Ranaldo 2009); order flow shows net purchases of foreign currency in own hours. Secondary description of windows: short EUR/USD ~03:00–09:00 ET (08:00–14:00 GMT), long ~11:00–15:00 ET (16:00–20:00 GMT). | Of the six pairs **only EUR/USD remains profitable after bid-ask spreads**; bp per day not reported in sources reached. | Significant. | Bid-ask spreads deducted. | **Implication**: the single best-documented intraday directional pattern for EUR/USD specifically; last tested data ends 2007 — must be re-validated.
- **Cornett, Schwarz & Szakmary (1995)**, "Seasonalities and intraday return patterns in the foreign currency futures market", *JBF* 19(5):843–869. | IMM futures 1977–1991. | Foreign currencies appreciate significantly during US trading hours. | Consistent with above. | Not reported.
- **Krohn, Mueller & Whelan (2024)**, "Foreign Exchange Fixings and Returns around the Clock", *J. Finance* 79(1):541–578 (BoC SWP 2021-48). | 9 currencies vs USD, 5-minute spot, around the clock. | USD appreciates gradually ahead of the Tokyo (09:55 JST), ECB (14:15 CET) and London WMR (16:00 London) fixes and reverts afterwards — a "W-shaped" daily pattern; after the London fix all currencies appreciate vs USD (yen the exception in one version). Strategy: short foreign currency pre-fix, long post-fix, net of costs. | Annualised net returns computed but not reported in sources reached; futures order imbalances unrelated to the pattern (intermediary-driven). | Significant. | Yes (net of transaction costs). | **Implication**: three daily USD-demand windows; EUR/USD should dip into 14:15 CET and 16:00 London and bounce after.
- **Evans (2018)**, "Forex Trading and the WMR Fix", *J. Banking & Finance* (MPRA 58151). | Intraday spot, many dollar pairs, post-2008 (exact sample not confirmed). | Returns negatively correlated either side of 16:00; reversal ≈ **2 bp in the first minute** after the fix, ≈ **5 bp total by 16:15**; larger at month-end. | Significant; intra-month reversals "not economically exploitable"; end-of-month strategy returns "one or two percent" (units unclear). | Costs considered. | **Implication**: fade the fix spike only at month-end.
- **Marsh, Panagiotou & Payne (2017)**, "The WMR Fix and its Impact on Currency Markets" (WP). | Spot + CME futures intraday. | Pre-fix accumulated positions reverse *during* the fix window in spot; reverse more slowly in futures. Related study: pre/post-fix moves relative to 15:45 ≈ **±15 bp at month-end vs ±7 bp intra-month**, with strong negative serial correlation on the last trading day. | Significant. | Not reported. | **Implication**: month-end 15:45–16:00 move then 16:00–16:15 reversal is the actionable version. **FCA Occasional Paper 46** reports price reversals around the fix *disappeared* after the 2015 widening of the window to 5 minutes — expect decay.
- **Ito & Yamada (2017)**, "Puzzles in the Tokyo Fixing", *JIE* 109:214–234 (NBER 22820); **Ito & Yamada (2015/2018)** "Was the Forex Fixing Fixed?"/"Did the Reform Fix the London Fix Problem?" *JIMF*. | USD/JPY tick data. | Pre-9:55 order flow biased toward USD buying → predictable price rise into the fix; bank fixing rates biased to USD appreciation; spikes more frequent than London. | Pip magnitudes not reported. | **Implication**: USD/JPY-specific, but consistent with KMW's cross-currency USD demand at 09:55 JST.
- **Ito & Hashimoto (2006)**, "Intraday seasonality in activities of the foreign exchange markets", *J. Japanese & Int. Economies* 20(4):637–664 (NBER 12413). | EBS USD/JPY and EUR/USD quotes and deals. | U-shaped activity within Tokyo and London sessions but **not** New York; activity and volatility positively correlated; volatility and spread negatively correlated; concentration in Tokyo–London and London–NY overlaps; spreads widest at Tokyo open (00:00 GMT). | Descriptive. | **Implication**: use an hour-of-day volatility/spread profile; avoid 21:00–00:00 GMT.
- **Krohn & Sushko (2020/2021)**, "FX spot and swap market liquidity spillovers", BIS WP 836. | Dealer-level spot and swap quotes. | Spot and swap bid-ask spreads highly correlated; quarter-end funding shocks widen spot spreads; top dealers withdraw from swaps around regulatory reporting dates. | Intraday spread levels not reported in sources reached. | **Implication**: raise cost assumptions at quarter-/year-end.
- Practitioner spread profile (not peer-reviewed; raw/ECN accounts): Asian session 0.3–1.2 pips, London 0.0–0.2, London–NY overlap 0.0–0.2, late NY wider.

## 5. Macro announcements and central banks

- **Andersen, Bollerslev, Diebold & Vega (2003)**, "Micro Effects of Macro Announcements: Real-Time Price Discovery in Foreign Exchange", *AER* 93(1):38–62. | 5-minute DM/$ (then EUR/$), £, ¥, CHF, 1992–1998 (six years), MMS survey consensus. | Return(t) regressed on lagged returns and standardised surprises (actual−consensus)/sd for ~40 US and German releases. | Announcement surprises produce conditional-mean **jumps** completed within minutes; responses to bad news larger than good news; payrolls among the strongest. Per-sd coefficients not reported in sources reached. A later Fed paper finds the payrolls sensitivity peaked in the early 1990s and declined since. | Highly significant. | n/a. | **Implication**: direction after a surprise is known (positive US surprise → EUR/USD down) but the move is over in ~5 minutes; only latency-sensitive strategies capture it.
- **Faust, Rogers, Wang & Wright (2007)**, "The high-frequency response of exchange rates and interest rates to macroeconomic announcements", *JME* 54(4):1051–1068 (IFDP 784). | 14 years, 1987–2002, 20-minute windows, DM/€, £, ¥. | Stronger-than-expected real-activity release appreciates USD and raises US yields; the implied path is consistent with Dornbusch overshooting (future expected depreciation). PPI effect larger pre-1992. | Significant; coefficients not reported in sources reached. | **Implication**: confirms ABDV signs; no exploitable drift after the window.
- **Mueller, Tahbaz-Salehi & Vedolin (2017)**, "Exchange Rates and Monetary Policy Uncertainty", *J. Finance* 72(3):1213–1252. | G10 vs USD, daily, scheduled FOMC days 1994–2013 (per WP). | Short USD / long basket on scheduled FOMC days. | Excess returns "an order of magnitude larger" on FOMC days than other days; larger for high-differential currencies, when MP uncertainty is high, and in easing cycles; much is **pre-announcement drift**. bp/day not reported in sources reached. | Significant. | Not reported. | **Implication**: long EUR/USD into scheduled FOMC days, unconditionally.
- **Karnaukh (2018 WP)**, "The Dollar Ahead of FOMC Target Rate Changes" (SSRN 3221318). | USD vs G10, 1994–2017 (per WP). | Over the **two days before** a scheduled FOMC meeting, USD rises ≈ **20 bp** when fed-funds futures signal a hike and falls ≈ **32 bp** (another draft: 25 bp) when they signal a cut; futures explain the drift with R² ≈ 22%; drift concentrated when FX implied vol is high. | Significant. | Not reported. | **Implication**: conditional pre-FOMC drift signed by futures-implied direction.
- **Lee & Wang (2025)**, "Jumps and Post-FOMC Announcement Returns in Currency Markets", *Review of Asset Pricing Studies* 15(3–4):247–287. | Intraday G10. | Post-FOMC returns are significantly negative (for the foreign currency), offsetting ≈ **65%** of the pre-FOMC drift, mostly 12–24 hours after the announcement. | Significant. | **Implication**: pre-FOMC long EUR/USD should be closed at the announcement; consider the reverse trade 12–24h later.
- **Lucca & Moench (2015)**, "The Pre-FOMC Announcement Drift", *J. Finance*: drift in equities only; "no FOMC pre-announcement effect in fixed income and foreign exchange." **Neuhierl et al. (2020)** and **Borisenko & Pozdeev** find a conditional FX drift (USD down before cuts, up before hikes; absent when no change). | **Implication**: the unconditional FX pre-FOMC drift is disputed; the conditional one (Karnaukh) is better supported.
- **Altavilla, Brugnolini, Gürkaynak, Motto & Ragusa (2019)**, "Measuring euro area monetary policy", *JME* 108:162–179 (ECB WP 2281; EA-MPD database). | Intraday, 2002–. | Press-release window (13:45 CET) carries only a Target surprise; press-conference window (14:30–15:30 CET) carries Timing, Forward-Guidance and QE factors. **Comazzi et al. (2026, VoxEU)**: 2y-OIS surprises explain ≈ **16%** of EUR/USD changes during ECB press conferences, 2002–2023; exchange-rate mentions add nothing. | **Implication**: trade the sign of the 2y OIS move during the press conference; ECB communication about the euro itself is not a signal.
- **Savor & Wilson (2013)**, *JFQA* 48(2): equity announcement-day premium (11.4 bp vs 1.1 bp). **Hu, Du & Zhao (JFR)**: currency risk premium higher on scheduled US announcement days. **FMG DP258** and **Norges Bank Staff Memo 5/2026**: FX news effects lose significance quickly as the window lengthens; adjustment happens on release. | **Implication**: no post-announcement drift in FX worth trading.

## 6. Calendar effects

- **Yamori & Kurihara (2004)**, "The day-of-the-week effect in foreign exchange markets: multi-currency evidence", *Research in International Business & Finance* 18(1):51–57. | 29 currencies, NY market, 1980s–1990s. | Day dummies on daily returns. | Effect present in the 1980s, **disappeared in the 1990s** for almost all (esp. European) currencies. **Yamori & Mourdoukoutas (2003)** same for ¥/$ (existed 1973–89, gone 1990s). | **Implication**: dead.
- **Bush & Stephens (IJFE)**, "The Return of the Monday Effect": significant Monday effect in three pairs 2010–2012, absent 1999–2004 and 2005–2009. **Grammatikos (1991)**: Monday *volatility* lowest in five currency futures. **Han, Kling & Sell (1999)**: day effects in futures volatility only. Seasonax (vendor, unverified): euro weak Mondays, strong Fridays since 2000. | **Implication**: at most a sporadic, regime-bound Monday effect; Friday effect unsupported academically.
- **Etula, Rinne, Suominen & Vaittinen (2020)**, "Dash for Cash: Monthly Market Impact of Institutional Liquidity Needs", *RFS* 33(1):75–111. | US stocks, bonds, money rates. | Selling T-8…T-4 before month-end, reversal T-3…T-1 and T+1 reinvestment; repo/LIBOR rise. **No FX result reported.** | **Implication**: untested in FX; a hypothesis only.
- **Month-end FX rebalancing**: no academic study found. Practitioner models (SocGen, Forex.com 2014–15) sign expected month-end USD flow by relative equity/bond performance, concentrated at the last-day 16:00 London fix; HSBC's Donnelly reports the classic month-end USD trade "stopped working" (arbitraged away). Peer-reviewed support is indirect (Evans; Marsh et al.: ±15 bp month-end fix moves).
- **Year-end**: CME (2022) shows USD funding pressure peaking ~1 week before year-end with the funding differential falling ~107 bp from mid-Dec peak to early Jan; **Du, Tepper & Verdelhan (2018)** *JF* document CIP basis widening at quarter/year-ends. No study links this to spot EUR/USD direction. | **Implication**: cost/liquidity effect, not a directional signal.
- **Weekend gaps** (Dao 2016, above): reversal after large Fri-close→Mon-open gaps in 15/16 pairs, 2002–2014. Practitioner stats (unverified): EUR/USD gaps on ~65% of Sunday opens; 204 gaps Jan 2001–May 2020; vendor claim of 88.4% 48-hour fill for gaps < 0.5×ATR.

## 7. Volatility

- **Plíhal & Lyócsa (2021)**, "Modeling realized volatility of the EUR/USD exchange rate: Does implied volatility really matter?", *Int. Review of Economics & Finance* 71:811–829. | EUR/USD 5-min RV and OTC implied vols (1-day to 1-month). | HAR-RV: RV(t+h) on daily, weekly, monthly RV averages, augmented with IV. | IV-augmented models improve OOS predictive accuracy by up to **17.3% (1-day), 42.1% (1-week), 22.8% (1-month)**; IV subsumes most of RV's information; gains stable over time and in high-vol periods. | Significant (MCS). | n/a. | **Implication**: for sizing/stops use HAR with 1-day/1-week implied vol if available; else plain HAR.
- **Lyócsa, Plíhal & Výrost (2024)**, *IJF* 40(4): day-ahead expected shortfall on EUR/USD — implied vol more valuable than realised measures.
- **arXiv 2506.07928 (2025)**, "Predicting Realized Variance Out of Sample: Can Anything Beat the Benchmark?" and a 2024 *DSFE* study on Dukascopy tick data 2010–2022: HAR tends to beat GARCH and stochastic-vol models OOS; averaging simple univariate forecasts helps. | **Implication**: HAR is the benchmark; GARCH(1,1) on daily closes is adequate but inferior.
- **Molnár (2012)**, "Properties of range-based volatility estimators", *IRFA* 23:20–29. | Simulation + data. | Relative efficiency vs close-to-close (=1): Parkinson 4.9, Garman-Klass 7.4, Meilijson 7.7 (2010 draft figures); square roots are biased for sd. | **Implication**: Garman-Klass on hourly/daily OHLC is the best cheap vol estimator; FX has no overnight gap problem except weekends.
- **Does volatility predict direction?** No paper found showing realised-vol level predicts EUR/USD sign. Related: **Della Corte, Ramadorai & Sarno (2016)** *JFE*: the currency volatility risk premium (IV−RV) predicts spot returns across up to 20 currencies, 1996–2011 (sell high-VRP, buy low-VRP); **Della Corte, Kozhan & Neuberger (2021)** *JFE* 139(3): volatility-carry in FX vol markets (long FVAs with downward-sloping IV curves); **Jurek (2009)** and BIS conference evidence: risk reversals track the forward bias and explain little of *future* skew; **Hoque, Le & Hassan (2020)**: the currency smirk predicts returns only over very short horizons; a semiskew-swap study finds far-tail information "contains no information" on future excess returns; **Ornelas (BCB WP 454)**: positive VRP–return relation across 32 currencies. | **Implication**: vol is for sizing; option skew is at best a very-short-horizon signal.

## 8. Fundamentals, order flow and positioning

- **Meese & Rogoff (1983)**: random walk beats structural models at 1–12 months. **Rossi (2013)**, "Exchange Rate Predictability", *JEL* 51(4):1063–1119: answer "it depends"; predictability most evident with **Taylor-rule or net-foreign-asset** predictors, linear models, few parameters; random walk **without drift** is the toughest benchmark; results depend on horizon, sample and test. | **Implication**: benchmark everything against RW-no-drift.
- **Molodtsova & Papell (2009)**, "Out-of-sample exchange rate predictability with Taylor rule fundamentals", *JIE* 77(2):167–180. | 12 OECD currencies vs USD, monthly, post-1973. | Forecast Δs from Taylor-rule differentials (inflation, output-gap, interest smoothing, heterogeneous coefficients). | Predictability for 11/12 currencies at 1-month (CW test); much stronger than PPP, monetary or interest-rate models. **Molodtsova, Nikolsko-Rzhevskyy & Papell**, "Taylor Rules and the Euro": €/$ 1999–2007 predictable with Taylor fundamentals, only in specs *without* the real exchange rate; **Ince & Molodtsova (2015)**: survives post-2008 with real-time data. | CW-statistic significant. | None (forecast evaluation). | **Implication**: a monthly fundamental overlay for EUR/USD with real-time ECB/Fed reaction-function inputs.
- **Engel & West (2005)**, "Exchange Rates and Fundamentals", *JPE* 113(3):485–517. | G7 vs USD quarterly 1974–2001. | With discount factor near 1 and I(1) fundamentals, the rate is near a random walk; exchange rates Granger-cause fundamentals (esp. nominal). Kent (2009) replication: causality found in 49% of tests. | **Implication**: do not expect fundamentals to forecast the level; the rate forecasts *them*.
- **Rogoff (1996)** PPP puzzle: real-rate half-life consensus 3–5 years (CI from ~1 year to infinity); panel/heterogeneity estimates as low as 11–18 months. | **Implication**: a multi-year mean-reversion anchor only; irrelevant at trading horizons.
- **Evans & Lyons (2002)**, "Order Flow and Exchange Rate Dynamics", *JPE* 110(1):170–180. | Reuters D2000-1 interdealer DM/$ daily, May–Aug 1996 (4 months). | Δs on signed interdealer order flow and interest differential. | R² > 60% (published; >50% in WP); $1bn net dollar purchases ≈ +0.5% (≈1 pfennig); OOS beats RW at short horizons. | Significant. | n/a. | **Implication**: contemporaneous order flow explains most daily variation but is unobservable to a retail system; CME 6E signed volume is the available proxy (untested here).
- **Klitgaard & Weir (2004)**, "Exchange Rate Changes and Net Positions of Speculators in the Futures Market", *FRBNY EPR* 10(1):17–28. | CFTC COT, 6 currencies, ~1993–2003 weekly. | Change in non-commercial net position vs same-week and next-week return. | Same-week sign agreement ~75%; **no predictive power for the following week**. | **Implication**: COT is a coincident, not leading, indicator.
- **Tornell & Yuan (2012)**, *J. Futures Markets* 32(2):122–151: findings not retrieved. **Hossfeld & Röthig (2016)**, "Do speculative traders anticipate or follow USD/EUR exchange rate movements?", *Finance Research Letters* 18:218–225 (Bundesbank DP 41/2015): predictive-regression evidence that EUR futures positions lead the rate "largely collapses" after FDR/FWER multiple-testing control; the rate leads positions. **RBNZ AN2018/03** reassesses COT similarly. | **Implication**: dead as a predictor.
- **Retail sentiment** (IG/DailyFX, FXCM SSI): no academic test found; purely practitioner "contrarian" convention. | **Implication**: null prior.

## 9. Machine learning on FX

- **Sezer, Gudelek & Ozbayoglu (2020)**, "Financial time series forecasting with deep learning: A systematic literature review 2005–2019", *Applied Soft Computing* 90:106181. | Taxonomy by asset and model; LSTM/GRU most common. Not a profitability audit; does not benchmark against RW after costs. | **Implication**: the mainstream literature mostly reports statistical metrics, not cost-adjusted trading results.
- **Federal Reserve FEDS 2025-089**: across samples, predictor sets and economic evaluation, "parsimony and the random-walk benchmark remain more reliable" than ML exchange-rate models.
- **arXiv 2606.15058 (2026)**, CAD/USD with expanding-window evaluation and DM tests: **only linear regression beats the random walk**; all ensemble ML models have negative DM statistics (worse than RW).
- **Amat et al. (2018)** (reviewed): OLS on Taylor-rule/fundamentals gives directional accuracy "over 50%" for most pairs.
- **EXFormer, arXiv 2512.12727 (2025)**: EUR/USD, USD/JPY, GBP/USD; authors report LSTM/Transformer baselines earn **negative cumulative returns after costs and slippage** while their model is positive, with directional-accuracy gains of 8.5–22.8% (self-reported; no multiple-testing control visible).
- **Dunis et al. (2011)**, *J. Derivatives & Hedge Funds*: MLP/RNN/HONN on daily EUR/USD vs RW and ARMA with costs, OOS Aug 2007–Feb 2009; conclusions not retrieved.
- Fischer & Krauss (2018) and Dixon et al. are equity/futures analogues; no FX-specific cost-adjusted replication found. | **Implication**: treat any DA > 53% on daily EUR/USD as suspect until it survives RW-no-drift, DM tests, costs, and a Reality-Check/SPA correction.

## 10. Market structure facts

- **BIS Triennial Survey (April 2022)**: global FX turnover **$7.5 trillion/day** (+14% vs 2019); USD on one side of **88%** of trades; EUR on one side of 30.5% (−1.8 pp vs 2019); EUR/USD the largest pair at **22.7%** of turnover (CME summary of BIS) ≈ $1.7 trn/day. Spot ≈ 28% of turnover (2022 release; exact split not retrieved).
- **CME Euro FX (6E)**: May 2025 ADV ≈ 184k contracts (~$27bn notional, +7% y/y); EUR/USD open-interest record 1m contracts on 17 June 2025; all CME FX futures 2025 average daily OI 2.4m contracts (~$228bn), Q4 2025 ~2.6m. Contract = €125,000; trades ~23 h/day with a 17:00–18:00 CT break; Sunday open 17:00 CT.
- **Session liquidity**: concentration in Tokyo–London and London–NY overlaps (Ito & Hashimoto); London–NY overlap (13:00–17:00 GMT) carries the highest volatility and tightest spreads; retail guides claim ~50% of daily volume in the overlap (unverified). Quarter-/year-end spread widening (Krohn & Sushko; CME turn data).
- **Weekend gaps**: EUR/USD gaps on ~65% of Sunday opens (forum, 18-month sample); 204 gaps 2001–2020 (Finance Magnates); most small gaps fill within days (vendor claims, unverified); large news-driven gaps extend (Dao 2016 nonetheless finds reversal).

## Ranked: 15 most robust, still-live effects (evidence-weighted)

1. **USD strength into the three daily fixes (Tokyo 09:55 JST, ECB 14:15 CET, London 16:00) and reversal after** — Krohn, Mueller & Whelan 2024 JF; 9 currencies, 5-min data, net of costs positive (magnitudes in paper, not retrieved). Most recent, top-journal, multi-currency.
2. **Conditional pre-FOMC dollar drift** — Karnaukh: ≈ +20 bp (hike-signalled) / −32 bp (cut-signalled) over the two days before the meeting, R² 22%; corroborated by Borisenko & Pozdeev.
3. **FOMC-day short-USD premium** — Mueller et al. 2017 JF: excess returns an order of magnitude larger on scheduled FOMC days.
4. **Post-FOMC reversal** — Lee & Wang 2025: ≈ 65% of the pre-FOMC drift is given back, mostly 12–24 h after.
5. **Month-end London-fix spike and reversal** — Evans 2018 (≈ 2 bp in 1 min, ≈ 5 bp by 16:15, larger at month-end); Marsh et al. (±15 bp month-end vs ±7 bp intra-month). Caveat: FCA finds reversals faded after the 2015 window change.
6. **Local-trading-hours depreciation (EUR weak 08:00–14:00 GMT, strong 16:00–20:00 GMT)** — Breedon & Ranaldo 2013; EUR/USD the only pair profitable net of spread, 1997–2007. Needs modern re-test.
7. **Macro-surprise jumps with known sign** — ABDV 2003, Faust et al. 2007: completed within ~5–20 minutes; exploitable only with sub-minute execution.
8. **12-month time-series momentum** — MOP 2012 (positive in all 58 contracts incl. 12 FX), AQR century evidence (combined net Sharpe 0.77); single-pair Sharpe low.
9. **1-month cross-sectional currency momentum** — Menkhoff et al. 2012 (up to 10% p.a. gross), BER 2011 (4.4% p.a., Sharpe 0.60); needs a G10 basket; post-GFC evidence mixed.
10. **Dollar carry / average forward discount** — LRV 2014: Sharpe > 0.5, 25% R² at 12 months; slow signal.
11. **Yield-curve changes (short-rate change, term-spread change)** — Ang & Chen 2010, Chen & Tsang 2013: predict excess FX returns up to 12–24 months, uncorrelated with carry.
12. **Taylor-rule fundamentals at monthly horizon** — Molodtsova & Papell 2009; €/$ 1999–2007 and post-crisis real-time (Ince & Molodtsova 2015).
13. **ECB press-conference 2y-OIS surprise → EUR/USD** — Altavilla et al. 2019; Comazzi et al. 2026: ~16% of press-conference variance.
14. **Volatility forecastability (HAR + implied vol)** — Plíhal & Lyócsa 2021: 17–42% OOS gains; use for sizing, stops and regime filters, not direction.
15. **Weekend-gap reversal** — Dao 2016: 15/16 pairs, 2002–2014; magnitudes unreported; cheap to test.

Honourable mention: currency volatility-risk-premium cross-section (Della Corte, Ramadorai & Sarno 2016) — needs option data on many currencies.

## Effects the evidence says are dead, never existed, or are not usable

- **Filter and moving-average rules on major pairs** — profits vanished by the early 1990s (Neely, Weller & Ulrich 2009; Qi & Wu 2006); LeBaron 1999 ties earlier profits to Fed intervention.
- **Day-of-week effects in FX** — gone since the 1990s (Yamori & Kurihara 2004; Yamori & Mourdoukoutas 2003); only a sporadic 2010–12 Monday effect (Bush & Stephens). Friday effect: no academic support.
- **CFTC COT positioning as a *leading* indicator** — Klitgaard & Weir 2004 (no next-week power); Hossfeld & Röthig 2016 (collapses under multiple-testing control; the rate leads positions).
- **Retail sentiment contrarian signals** — no academic evidence at all.
- **Simple month-end "buy/sell USD" trade** — practitioner (HSBC) reports it stopped working; no academic support beyond fix-window microstructure.
- **Unconditional pre-FOMC drift in FX** — Lucca & Moench 2015 find none (only the conditional version survives).
- **Post-announcement drift in FX** — effects lose significance beyond short windows (FMG DP258; Norges Bank 2026).
- **Carry on EUR/USD via the forward-premium puzzle post-2008** — Fama β turned positive ("New Fama Puzzle"); carry performance poor since the crisis.
- **Monetary/PPP structural models at horizons < 1 year** — Meese & Rogoff 1983; Rossi 2013 (RW-no-drift remains the toughest benchmark).
- **Volatility level predicting direction; risk reversals predicting direction beyond very short horizons** — no supportive study; Jurek, semiskew evidence negative.
- **LSTM/deep-learning directional models beating the random walk after costs** — no robust, snooping-corrected evidence (Fed FEDS 2025-089; arXiv 2606.15058); self-reported wins (EXFormer) unreplicated.
- **Intra-month fix reversals** — Evans 2018: not economically exploitable; FCA: reversals faded after 2015.
- **"Dash for cash" month-end pattern** — documented for US stocks/bonds only (Etula et al. 2020); untested in FX.
